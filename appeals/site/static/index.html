Vermont housing appeals · research data

What happens when someone challenges a Vermont town's decision on new housing?

When a town approves or turns down a housing project, the builder, neighbors and others can ask a court to review the decision. We collected every published ruling on those cases and recorded, in plain language, who appealed, what they argued, what the court decided and how long it took. Each fact links to the passage in the ruling it came from.

About this data

01

Summary

Building new homes in Vermont usually requires a permit from the town. When a town makes that decision, people who disagree with it can appeal to a court. This site is a record of those appeals: what was proposed, who objected, what the court decided and how long it took.

We built it by collecting the court's published rulings and reading each one carefully with the help of AI. Every fact we report is backed by a quotation from the ruling, which you can read for yourself on the case's page.

02

How appeals work

Most housing in Vermont needs local approval: a zoning permit, a subdivision approval or a similar permit. Applications are decided by a town official or a volunteer board, such as a development review board.

  1. Someone applies.A property owner or builder asks the town for permission to build homes, convert a building or divide land into lots.
  2. The town decides.It approves the project, approves it with conditions (for example, limits on size or hours of construction), or turns it down.
  3. Someone appeals.If the applicant, a neighbor or another party disagrees, they can take the decision to court. This is the appeal.
  4. The court rules.A judge reviews the project, usually from scratch, and can approve it, deny it, change the conditions or send it back to the town. Many appeals end earlier: they are dismissed, settled or withdrawn.

In Vermont these appeals go to the Environmental Division of the Vermont Superior Court, a part of the state court system that handles land use and environmental cases. People often call it the environmental court. Its cases can take months or years, and a single case can produce several written rulings along the way.

03

Sources

The only source is the court's own written rulings, which it publishes on the Vermont Judiciary's website. Each ruling is a document, often several pages long, in which the judge explains what the case is about and what the court decided.

We collected every ruling the Environmental Division has posted since 2000, about 2,560 documents, and grouped them into cases by their docket number, the number a court gives each case. That gives about 1,700 cases. Most are not about new housing: they concern state permits, enforcement, or projects with no homes. About 340 are appeals of a town's decision on new housing, and those are what this data describes.

04

Method

Reading thousands of court documents by hand would take years, so we used AI models: software that can read text and answer questions about it. To keep them accurate, we set firm rules.

  • The same questions for every case. The models answered a fixed list of questions, mostly multiple choice, such as What did the town decide? or Who filed the appeal?
  • Every answer needs a quotation. The model had to copy the sentence in the ruling that supports each answer. A separate program, not an AI, checked that the sentence really appears on that page. Answers that failed the check were discarded.
  • Not stated is allowed. When the rulings don't say something, we record that rather than guess.
  • Tested against reviewed answers. Before publishing, we compared the results with a set of cases whose answers had been carefully reviewed, and used the differences to improve the questions.

05

Recorded facts

Every appeal has its own page, with the facts below and the quotations behind them. You can also browse and search all of them, or ask an AI assistant to analyze them for you.

The project
What the permit would allow (new buildings, converted buildings or new lots), the kind of permit, and the town.
The town's decision
Whether the town approved the project, approved it with conditions, approved part of it, or denied it, and when.
Who appealed
Each person or group who appealed (the applicant, neighbors, an organization, the town or a state agency) and whether they had a lawyer.
What they argued
The issues in dispute, such as the character of the neighborhood, traffic, stormwater, building height or setbacks, or whether the appeal was filed properly, and how far the court got with each.
What the court decided
How the case ended, what happened to the project, why an appeal was dismissed, and whether the court's result differed from the town's.
How long it took
The number of days from the start of the appeal to the court's final decision. If no final decision has been published, we give the time to the latest ruling and mark it at least.
New homes
How many new homes the project proposed, how many the town approved and how many the court approved, plus any new building lots.

06

Limitations

  • Only cases with a published ruling. Appeals that ended without one, for example because they were withdrawn early, don't appear.
  • Whether homes were built. The rulings say what was permitted, not what happened afterwards.
  • Later appeals. Some cases go on to the Vermont Supreme Court; we stop at the Environmental Division's final decision.
  • An open case isn't proven open. If we found no final decision, the case may still have ended without one being published.
  • The same project can appear twice. When one ruling covers several dockets, each docket lists the same project, so adding up homes across them can double-count.
  • Mistakes are possible. AI models did the reading. The quotations are there so you can check any answer yourself.

07

Definitions

Appeal
A request for a court to review a town's decision.
Appellant
The person or group who files the appeal.
Docket
A court case, identified by its docket number, such as 25-ENV-00114.
Ruling or decision
A written document in which the judge explains what the court decided. One case can have several.
Conditions
Requirements attached to an approval, such as a limit on height or a required landscape buffer.
Sent back
The court returned the application to the town to decide again (a remand).
Dismissed
The court ended the appeal without deciding the project itself, for example because it was filed late.
Not stated
The published rulings don't say. This is different from no.
Version
Each release of the data has a number, such as v0.0.2, so results can be cited and compared.
