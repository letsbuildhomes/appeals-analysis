When a Vermont town decides on new housing, people can appeal that decision to the Environmental Division of the Vermont Superior Court. This project reads the court's published decisions and turns each case into a row of plain facts: what was proposed, who appealed, on what grounds, what the court did and how long it took. Every answer comes with a quote from the decision it came from.

What the data is

Who is counted. Appeals to the Environmental Division of the Vermont Superior Court against a town's decision about new housing, as seen through the decisions the court has published. New housing means the permission at issue lets someone build, place or convert a building that will contain homes, or create lots for housing. Turning a non-residential space into homes counts, with or without construction. Enforcement cases, changes of use that create no homes, accessory work and permits for something already built don't count, and neither do Act 250 and other state permit appeals, even when the project is housing.

One row per docket. A docket is one case number, such as 25-ENV-00114. Every docket the court has a decision for is read and labeled in scope or out of scope; only the in-scope ones get the full set of facts.

What each row tells you

Scope
What kind of proceeding the docket is, what the permission allows, and whether it adds homes, which together decide whether it is a municipal appeal about new housing. Also the kinds of permission at issue, such as subdivision, site plan, conditional use or a variance.
The town's decision
What the last town body decided (approved, approved with conditions, approved in part, or denied), and when. The town, and the village or area within it where the decisions name one.
Who appealed
Each appellant's role: the applicant or owner, a neighbor, a group of neighbors, another organization, the town or a state agency. And whether each had a lawyer.
The grounds
What the appeal argued about, and how far the court got with each issue. Some standards leave the outcome to judgment, such as the character of the area, effects on neighbors, traffic and services, or water and the environment. Others are questions such as measurable rules (setbacks, height, density), whether a use is allowed, who has the right to appeal, or how the town ran its process. Also whether anyone challenged a condition of the permit, and what the court did with it.
What the court did
How the docket ended (decided on the merits, dismissed, settled or withdrawn), what the court did with the application (granted, granted with conditions, granted in part, denied, or sent back to the town), why a dismissed appeal was dismissed, and whether the court ended somewhere different from the town. Also whether the court stayed the permit or changed the size or design of the buildings.
How long it took
Days from the filing of the appeal (or the town's decision, when no filing date is given) to the court's first decision that leaves nothing pending. When the record shows no such decision, the figure runs to the latest order and counts as at least that long.
Units and lots
How many new homes the project would add: as proposed, as the town approved and as the court approved. And how many new building lots for housing it would create. Only new homes are counted, not existing ones that stay.

Every answer shows its source. Ask about a case and the assistant can show the passage of the decision each answer rests on, with its page and a link to the PDF. Not stated is a real answer: it means the decisions were silent, which is different from no.

What it can't tell you

  • Only published decisions. Appeals that left no published decision are invisible here.
  • A missing final decision doesn't prove a case is still open. Many cases end without a decision that says so.
  • Nothing shows whether homes were built, or whether an appeal delayed construction.
  • Cases end at the Environmental Division. Appeals to the Vermont Supreme Court aren't followed.
  • Linked cases can describe the same project. A decision issued in several dockets belongs to each, so adding up homes across linked dockets can count the same homes twice.
  • AI models read the decisions, and they can be wrong. The quotes are there so you can check.

How it works

  1. DocumentsEvery decision the Environmental Division has posted on the court's website since 2000, about 2,560 PDFs, with their text read from the page (or scanned with OCR).
  2. DocketsThe documents are grouped by case number into about 1,760 dockets, one per case.
  3. A fast first readA fast decision model reads every docket and answers dozens of multiple-choice questions at once, each with a probability.
  4. Careful questionsFor the dockets likely in scope, a reasoning model answers one small question at a time. Each answer must quote the decision, and code checks the quote.
  5. VersionsCode combines the answers into research facts, frozen into a numbered version, such as v0.0.1: the data your assistant reads.

Ideas to start chatting

Paste any of these into your assistant once it is connected.

  • How many housing appeals does the data cover, and over which years? Which version is it?

  • How long do housing appeals usually take, from filing to the court's final decision? Has that changed over the years? Keep cases with no final decision separate.

  • Who files these appeals? How often is it neighbors challenging an approval, and how often is it the developer challenging a denial?

  • Which towns have had the most housing appeals? Show the top ten.

  • How often does the court end up somewhere different from the town's decision, and in which direction?

  • What do appeals most often argue about: traffic, the character of the neighborhood, stormwater, setbacks? How often does the court decide those disputes?

  • How many housing appeals were dismissed, and why: standing, timeliness, or something else?

  • How often were appellants represented by a lawyer?

  • Find the appeals about projects in Burlington and tell me, with a quote from each decision, what the court decided.

One piece of advice: ask the assistant to name the version and which cases it counted. Not stated means the decisions were silent, not no, so ask it to report those separately.

The source is the court's own published decisions, at vtcourts.gov. For developers: the same data is available as read-only JSON at {{BASE}}/api/releases.
